China's Courts Drew a Line: AI Is Not Grounds for Dismissal

Wiba Signals · 20 May 2026 · The Worker
  • Two Chinese courts have ruled that companies cannot dismiss workers solely because AI can now perform their role. Legal principle established: AI adoption is a strategic business choice, not a force majeure event.
  • In the same period, 78,000–92,000 US tech workers were laid off in the first four months of 2026. Nearly half of those cuts were explicitly attributed to AI. No equivalent legal protection exists in the United States.
  • The divergence is structural, not coincidental. China and the US are building different legal frameworks for the same technological transition — with opposite default assumptions about who bears the risk.

The Rulings

In Hangzhou, a quality assurance supervisor at an AI company — identified in court documents as "Joe" — was terminated after his employer concluded that its own AI systems had improved to the point where his role was redundant. Joe earned approximately 25,000 yuan per month (roughly $3,600) reviewing and optimising AI-generated outputs. The company argued the position had become unnecessary.

The court rejected that argument. Its ruling established a legal principle with broad implications: AI adoption is a strategic business choice, not an unforeseeable change in circumstances. Under Chinese labour law, unforeseeable circumstances can justify dismissal. A deliberate technology investment made by the company itself does not qualify as unforeseeable. Therefore, the company — not the employee — bears the cost of the transition it chose to make.

A second case in a separate Chinese jurisdiction reached the same conclusion through the same legal logic.

The US Contrast

Between January and April 2026, an estimated 78,000 to 92,000 tech workers were laid off in the United States. Reporting from multiple outlets indicates that nearly half of those layoffs were attributed — explicitly, in company statements or filings — to AI-driven efficiency gains and headcount restructuring.

No equivalent legal framework governs AI-related dismissals in the US. Employment at will remains the dominant doctrine in most states, meaning workers can be let go for any reason not specifically prohibited by law. AI-driven restructuring does not fall into any protected category.

The difference is not primarily about worker protection philosophy. It is about who the legal system designates as the risk-bearer when a company makes a technology choice that displaces employees. Chinese courts have begun resolving this question in one direction. US courts and legislators have not yet been asked to resolve it at all.

What the Principle Means for Businesses Outside China

The Hangzhou ruling does not apply beyond China's jurisdiction. But the underlying legal argument it established — that AI adoption is a chosen strategy, not an act of God — is structurally portable. The same argument could be made before labour tribunals in Germany, France, or Sweden, where worker protections are more robust and the concept of "socially justified dismissal" already places constraints on technology-driven restructuring.

EU member states operate under different national implementations of the EU's collective redundancy directive. Whether AI-driven headcount reductions qualify as economic, technical, or structural grounds for redundancy — and whether companies have adequate consultation obligations before making those cuts — has not yet been tested in high-profile cases in most European jurisdictions.

That legal gap is narrowing. The Chinese rulings are, at minimum, a signal of which direction one major jurisdiction has moved — and a preview of arguments likely to appear elsewhere.
