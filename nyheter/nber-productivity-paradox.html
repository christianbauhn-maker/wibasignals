The Productivity Paradox Is a Measurement Problem, Not an AI Problem

Wiba Signals · 16 May 2026 · The Worker

A new study surveyed 6,000 executives across the US, UK, Germany, and Australia on AI's impact in their organisations. The finding: 89% reported no measurable productivity change.

Headlines ran with it. "AI Hype Doesn't Deliver." "The AI Productivity Boom That Wasn't."

Here's the problem: the study is methodologically sound. And it is measuring the wrong thing for roughly half the workforce.

What the Study Actually Measures

The NBER working paper (34836, Yotzov, Barrero et al., November 2025–January 2026) uses a clean primary metric: volume of sales per employee.

That measure works well if AI's value shows up as increased output — more deals closed, more units produced, more revenue generated per head. For some roles, that is exactly what happens. A designer who used to deliver 10 concepts per week can now deliver 40. A developer who wrote 200 lines of clean code per day can write 600. The output scales, it is measurable, and it shows up in the numbers.

But for the majority of knowledge workers — coordinators, advisors, analysts, case workers, educators, managers — work does not function that way. These roles have a natural ceiling. A welfare advisor handles a fixed number of cases per day. A HR manager can only attend so many onboarding meetings. A school counsellor can only see so many students.

For these roles, AI does not produce more output. It produces something else entirely.

The Distinction the Numbers Miss

There are two fundamentally different types of work in any organisation.

Production work is directly output-correlated: it scales if the bottleneck is repetitive execution. AI in production roles generates more deliverables, faster, at lower marginal cost. This shows up cleanly in sales-per-employee metrics.

Fundamentals work is the operational layer that makes production possible — scheduling, documentation, reporting, standard communications, information processing. It has a structural ceiling set by the role's nature, not by the employee's capacity.

When AI automates fundamentals work, it does not raise the ceiling. It creates a time surplus. Meetings are prepared faster. Reports take thirty minutes instead of three hours. Documentation happens in real time instead of Friday afternoon.

That surplus goes somewhere — into client relationships, into cognitive breathing room, into actually thinking instead of just processing. It is real value. It simply does not appear as "sales per employee."

A measurement framework built for factory floors does not translate to knowledge work. That is not an indictment of the NBER study. It is a limitation that the study's authors acknowledge, and one that aggregate data cannot resolve.

What This Means in Practice

If you are evaluating AI's impact in your organisation using revenue metrics alone, you are getting an accurate answer to an incomplete question.

The more useful diagnostic is structural: what proportion of each role consists of repeatable, predictable tasks? Where does the time surplus go when those tasks are automated? Does the freed capacity flow into higher-value work — or does it get silently absorbed by higher volume expectations?

That last question matters most. Time saved by AI has a way of disappearing into additional workload rather than genuine capacity gain. Organisations that treat the surplus as an input to more output will measure no productivity effect. Organisations that deliberately redirect it — into quality, into relationships, into development — will see it eventually. Just not in next quarter's sales figures.

The paradox is not that AI does not work.

It is that we built our scorecards before we understood what it actually changes.

Source: NBER Working Paper 34836, Yotzov, Barrero, Bloom, Davis, Meyer & Mihaylov (2026). Survey of approximately 6,000 senior managers across the US, UK, Germany, and Australia, conducted November 2025–January 2026. Self-reported data. Pre-peer review at time of publication.
