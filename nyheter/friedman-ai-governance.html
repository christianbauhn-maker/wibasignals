Friedman: AI Governance Needs a Nixon-China Moment

Wiba Signals · 20 May 2026 · The Market
  • Tom Friedman, writing in the New York Times and speaking on CNBC, argues that AI governance requires US-China cooperation comparable in geopolitical significance to Nixon's 1972 opening with China — and that adversarial competition without governance frameworks risks outcomes neither side can control.
  • The proximate cause: Anthropic's unreleased "Mythos" model, which demonstrated the ability to autonomously identify and exploit vulnerabilities in code at a speed, scope, and scale no human analyst could match — capability that a 15-year-old with a laptop and internet connection could in principle direct against critical infrastructure.
  • Sentiment shift among AI industry insiders: investor Paul Tudor Jones, speaking on CNBC, reported that at a major 2026 AI conference, approximately 80% of attendees expressed support for government regulation — compared to roughly 20% at the equivalent gathering one year prior. This figure is attributed to Jones's observation and has not been independently verified by Wiba Signals.

The Geopolitical Framing

Friedman's framing in his New York Times column is that the world is in a "phase change" — moving from an age of information, where tools extended individual reach but required human expertise to operate, to an age of intelligence, where systems act autonomously on intent expressed in natural language.

That transition changes the risk calculus of the US-China technology competition. In the information age, competition was about who had better tools. In the intelligence age, competition is about who can constrain adversarial uses of tools that both sides possess — and whether both sides can agree to constrain them before the capabilities become widely accessible.

Friedman characterises this as analogous to the early nuclear period: a moment where the technology's power was clear, its governance was undetermined, and the outcome depended on whether rival powers could cooperate on constraints before the window for doing so closed.

The Mythos Signal

Anthropic developed a model — referred to internally as "Mythos" — that demonstrated autonomous vulnerability discovery and exploitation in software systems. The company chose not to release it. OpenAI has developed comparable capability. Google DeepMind has capability in this area as well.

Friedman's point is that the restraint of major AI labs is not a stable governance mechanism. Labs that choose not to release dangerous capability today can be acquired, hacked, or outcompeted by actors that make different choices tomorrow. The meaningful constraint is not corporate policy but international agreement — the kind that requires bilateral negotiation, not unilateral commitment.

The argument parallels the case made for nuclear non-proliferation: the technology itself cannot be uninvented, but its deployment can be constrained by international framework if states act before proliferation makes framework-building impossible.

The Sentiment Shift

At a May 2026 conference attended by approximately 50 senior figures across the AI industry — investors, lab leaders, chip manufacturers, and application builders — investor Paul Tudor Jones reported that approximately 80% of attendees now support government regulation of AI. One year earlier, at the equivalent gathering, the proportion was approximately 20%.

That 60-percentage-point shift in twelve months among a population of people who are financially invested in AI's continued growth is a significant signal. Whether it translates into active lobbying for regulation, acceptance of regulatory frameworks when proposed, or simply reflexive endorsement in conference settings is a separate question. But the stated position of the industry's own major players has moved substantially toward regulation — and away from the "move fast" consensus that characterised the 2022–2024 period.
