42% of Recent US Graduates Are Underemployed — The Highest Rate Since 2020

Wiba Signals · 20 May 2026 · The Worker
  • US unemployment among college graduates now equals or exceeds that of non-graduates — a reversal with no recorded historical precedent, according to Andrew Yang, citing current labour market data.
  • Underemployment among recent college graduates has reached 42% — the highest rate since 2020, per Federal Reserve Bank of New York data (Q1 2026). CS-programme graduates are experiencing the sharpest decline in hiring rates among all degree holders.
  • Dario Amodei, CEO of Anthropic, has stated publicly and repeatedly that AI will automate 50% of entry-level white-collar jobs within several years. He has separately called for AI companies to be taxed as a structural response — a position he described as recognition that "the writing is on the wall."

The Reversal

The labour market premium attached to a university degree in the United States has been durable across most of the post-war period. College graduates have consistently shown lower unemployment rates than non-graduates, higher wage growth, and higher lifetime earnings. That premium has narrowed in recent cycles. As of 2026, it has effectively disappeared on unemployment metrics.

Andrew Yang, speaking at a CNBC interview in May 2026, cited the current underemployment rate among recent college graduates as above 50% — meaning more than half of recent graduates are working in roles that do not require their degree or are not working at all. He described the unemployment rate among college graduates as "the same or higher than non-college graduates for the first time in history."

Wiba Signals has not independently verified the exact current BLS figures underlying this characterisation. The directional claim — that the graduate premium is compressing or reversing — is consistent with trends documented in recent Federal Reserve and Census Bureau labour market analyses.

The Computer Science Signal

The sharpest decline is visible in computer science graduate hiring. Yang cited one company in the autonomous coding space — selling AI-driven enterprise development tools — whose revenue grew 100-fold in the preceding 12 months. That growth represents budget that previously paid human developers now flowing to automated systems.

The cohort most affected is not senior engineers with ten or fifteen years of experience. It is recent graduates entering the workforce, and the internship and entry-level positions they historically occupied. Companies that would have hired a cohort of junior developers for training-to-production pipelines are instead contracting AI coding agents. The pipeline has shortened, not eliminated human developers — but it has eliminated the on-ramp.

Four years ago, "learn to code" was the consensus career advice for young people seeking economic security. The consensus has reversed with unusual speed.

The Amodei Position

Dario Amodei has made the same forecast in multiple public forums: AI will automate approximately 50% of entry-level white-collar work within several years. He has been specific that "the easiest people to fire are the people you haven't hired yet" — meaning the displacement shows up in hiring contraction before it shows up in layoff statistics.

His call for AI companies to bear tax obligations proportional to the displacement they cause is unusual for a technology CEO. Yang described it as evidence that Amodei "sees the writing on the wall" and anticipates a significant political and social backlash. Whether tax policy will follow is a separate question. That the CEO of one of the leading AI developers is making this argument publicly is itself a signal about where the industry privately locates the risk.
