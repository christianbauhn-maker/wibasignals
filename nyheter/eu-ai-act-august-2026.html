EU AI Act: The August 2026 Deadline Companies Are Missing

Wiba Signals · 20 May 2026 · The Machine
  • The EU AI Act's high-risk obligations take effect August 2026. These cover AI systems used in hiring decisions, credit scoring, medical technology, education, and critical infrastructure — among other categories. Penalties for high-risk violations reach €15 million or 3% of global annual turnover. The maximum tier (€35M, 7%) applies to violations of the Act's outright bans — a separate and higher category.
  • The Act applies extraterritorially: any company offering AI-enabled services to EU customers is subject, regardless of where the company is headquartered. This includes US and non-EU SaaS providers whose tools are used by EU-based organisations.
  • There are two regulated roles: providers (companies that build and sell AI tools) and deployers (companies that use someone else's AI tool in their operations). Most businesses — including the vast majority of SMEs — are deployers, and most deployers have not started high-risk compliance assessments.

The Risk Framework

The EU AI Act classifies AI systems across four risk tiers. At the top, a small set of AI applications are banned outright: social scoring by governments, real-time biometric surveillance in public spaces, and emotion recognition in workplaces or schools. These prohibitions have been in effect since February 2025.

The tier that affects the largest number of organisations is high-risk. High-risk AI systems are those whose decisions carry significant consequences for individuals — whether in employment, finance, healthcare, education, law enforcement, or critical infrastructure. For these systems, the Act requires:

  • A documented risk management system
  • Clean, relevant, and unbiased training data
  • Detailed technical documentation and audit logs
  • Human oversight capability — a designated person who can override the AI's output
  • Registration in the EU's AI database before deployment

For AI systems used in recruitment — CV screening, candidate ranking, interview scoring — this means a company cannot simply deploy an off-the-shelf ATS with AI features and proceed. They must document it, register it, and demonstrate that a human can and does override its outputs.

The Deployer Distinction

The most important distinction for most organisations is the provider/deployer split. A provider builds and sells an AI product. A deployer purchases or licenses it and uses it in their operations.

Deployers bear their own compliance obligations under the Act — separate from, and in addition to, whatever obligations apply to the tool's provider. A company that uses an AI-powered HR platform to screen candidates is a deployer of a high-risk system and must meet the high-risk requirements independently, even if the platform vendor itself is compliant.

This is the gap that the IAPP (International Association of Privacy Professionals) identified in its assessment of European business readiness: most deployers have not conducted an AI inventory, have not classified their systems by risk tier, and have no audit trail for AI-assisted decisions.

The Timeline in Practice

The EU AI Act's rollout has been phased. Outright bans took effect February 2025. General-purpose AI model obligations (covering large foundation models like GPT-4, Claude, and Gemini) took effect August 2025. High-risk system obligations are the next and most operationally complex phase — effective August 2026.

The Omnibus revision process extended some compliance timelines into late 2027 for certain categories. But Articles 4 and 5 — covering prohibited practices and the general obligation to implement appropriate AI literacy measures for staff — are already in effect. Organisations that have done nothing are not in a waiting period; they are already non-compliant on baseline provisions.

The enforcement gap between a regulation taking effect and the first significant fine being issued is typically 12–18 months in EU regulatory history. That window is narrowing.
