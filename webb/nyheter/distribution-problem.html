The Market

The Distribution Problem

Wiba Signals · 13 May 2026 · The Worker

Sweden holds a position most countries would envy. According to Stanford HAI's 2024 AI Index, Sweden ranks first globally in AI talent concentration — 1.98% of LinkedIn members with AI skills, ahead of the US, Israel, and Singapore. In EU-wide adoption rankings, Sweden sits third, with 35% of businesses using AI compared to the EU average of 20%.

On paper, Sweden is an AI leader. In practice, the picture is considerably more complicated.

The Numbers Behind the Narrative

Swedish statistics agency SCB published its IT-use-among-businesses survey in December 2025. The findings are precise enough to be uncomfortable.

Among large Swedish companies — those with 250 or more employees — AI adoption stands at 71.9%. That is a number competitive with any economy in the world. Among small companies, those with 10 to 49 employees, adoption falls to 30.8%. And 57.9% of all Swedish businesses — across all size categories — neither use AI nor are actively considering it.

Sweden is not lagging behind Europe. But two out of three Swedish businesses are outside the transition entirely.

What Is Blocking the Other 57.9%

SCB asked the businesses that considered AI but chose not to proceed what stopped them. 74.7% cited a lack of expertise.

Not cost. Not regulation. Not a principled decision that AI is not relevant to their industry. A straightforward shortage of people who know how to implement it.

This is the distribution problem in precise form: Sweden produces world-class AI talent, and that talent is concentrated in companies large enough to attract, retain, and pay for it. The knowledge does not reach smaller businesses — not because smaller businesses are less capable of using AI effectively, but because the mechanism for transferring that knowledge has not been built.

Why This Gap Is Not Closing on Its Own

Large companies adopt AI through dedicated teams, vendor relationships, and enterprise agreements. Their path to implementation is paved with resources most SMEs do not have access to.

Small and medium-sized businesses face a different problem. The same OECD report on bridging the AI skills gap (April 2025) found that one in three job vacancies in high-exposure occupations now requires AI literacy — but training systems are systematically underserving the SME segment. Enterprise-oriented programmes, consultant day rates sized for large procurement budgets, and implementation timelines that assume internal IT capacity are all designed for a different buyer.

The expertise exists in Sweden. It simply is not where the gap is.

The Actual Question

The debate about whether Sweden is ahead or behind on AI is a distraction. The more productive question is whether the 71.9% adoption rate in large companies will compound into a structural advantage — or whether the 57.9% that have not started will find the gap too wide to close by the time they decide to try.

That is not a prediction. It is the question that the data makes unavoidable.
